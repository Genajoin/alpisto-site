The [previous article](/blog/how-a-flybeeper-vario-decides-when-to-beep) was about when a vario
beeps: the averaging, two thresholds on each side, a memory of which tone is playing and an early
exit. It ended with seven presets for those thresholds. This one is about the other half, how the
beep sounds, and about what happened when both halves went into one list.

The reason is simple. A pilot who has flown five years with one vario has that vario's sound in
their head. They know what +0.5 sounds like and what +3 sounds like without looking. A new vario
with a different curve is a new language in the one moment you least want to learn one. So the
FlyBeeper app now lets you pick a ready sound, including sounds as close as we could get to the
varios and flight apps people already fly with. Moving to a FlyBeeper no longer means learning to
hear the air again.

In the previous article I left the brand names out of the table: the point there was the numbers.
Here the names are the point. They belong to their owners, none of whom endorsed anything below.

## One list instead of three

Until now the sound settings had a strip of tone curves, a grid of "When it sounds" presets and two
separate menus for your own profiles. They are now one list in the Sound section of a device's
settings in the app, in four groups: our own sounds, sounds of other varios and apps, presets from
the community, and threshold sets on their own.

An entry carries a tone curve, thresholds, or both. Above the list a switch says what a choice
changes: everything, only the sound, or only when it sounds. So you can take the climb tone of one
vario and the thresholds of another, and the app recognises the two parts separately: the line
under the list reads like "Sound: SkyDrop · When it sounds: Sniffer". Own brings back the values
you had before you started clicking. A star marks a favourite. Tap an entry and its description
opens right under it: what it sounds like and the thresholds in numbers.

Everything runs on the same twelve-point curve the previous article described: for each of twelve
vario values, a pitch, a period and a share of the period that sounds. Between the points the vario
draws straight lines. Whatever another vario does, it has to fit into that.

## Where the numbers come from

Some makers publish their tone tables, and a few do it in a format that is almost ours: a climb rate,
a pitch, a period and a duty for each of a dozen points. Open-source varios and flight apps have
their curves in the code. Pilots share their own tuned profiles, and some of those files have been
passed around for years.

For the rest, there is more out there than one would think. Manuals describe the sound in words and
charts, forum threads argue over single points of a curve, settings screens show their defaults, and
pilots post videos where a vario beeps through a whole climb with its display in view. Taken
together, that is enough to rebuild some popular curves that were never published as numbers. Those
entries are marked in the app as partly rebuilt.

Two things never come across. Many varios use a table longer than twelve points; where one did, we
kept the points whose loss changes the curve most and dropped the rest. And nobody states their
averaging in seconds: every manual has its own damping scale. So the entries of other varios leave
the averaging as it is on your device, and the thresholds and the curve do the rest.

One entry deserves a word of its own. Bräuniger's IQ varios, sold also as Flytec, were on the
harness of a whole generation of pilots, and many schools taught on them. Their sound is often
called the standard: a climb tone from 700 Hz that rises steadily, fast beeps of exactly half a beat
that get faster with the climb, and a steady sink tone that drops as the sink grows. The manuals
describe the factory steps of each setting, and the Bräuniger IQ entry plays those steps.

Two more of our own entries are about loudness, not melody. A FlyBeeper instrument carries one of two
piezo buzzers, one loudest around 4 kHz and one around 2 kHz. A piezo is loudest at its resonant
frequency, so Buzzer 4 kHz and Buzzer 2 kHz put the whole sound right at that resonance and get the
most volume the piezo can give. The rhythm and the half-beat beep are the same as in Simple below.
It is higher than the usual vario sound, and in a strong wind or under a thick helmet that is the
point.

## Simple

The first entry in the list is ours, and it is the one I would give a pilot who has no sound in
their head yet. It keeps the three zones that most varios share, a steady sink tone, quiet ticks
near zero and climb beeps, and gives each zone one rule instead of hand-tuned points.

The climb starts at 600 Hz and goes 150 Hz higher for each metre per second, up to 1050 Hz at +3,
then flattens to 1550 Hz at +10. That keeps the whole climb on the part of our buzzer where loudness
is even, below the dip around 1600–1800 Hz that the [piezo article](/blog/vario-piezo-tone-measured)
measured. The beep is always half of the period, and the period goes from 600 ms to 150 ms, the
rhythm nearly every vario on the market uses. The sink tone is steady and falls with the sink, from
580 Hz to 400 Hz. The ticks near zero, from −0.5 to +0.04, sit below the climb tone's hold of +0.05,
so with Simple's own thresholds they never sound; pick Sniffer or Flatland under When it sounds and
they do.

The thresholds are what most of the market does: climb from +0.1, held to +0.05, sink below −2.5, no
early exit. The averaging, 0.3 s, is calmer than our factory 0.1 s and gives fewer false beeps in
choppy air, without the lag of the 0.6 s in Mountain.

## The phone beeps with the instrument, not after it

Most flight apps can make a vario sound from an external instrument, and most of them share one
weakness: the app's beep comes noticeably later than the instrument's own. The instrument hears a
thermal first, then its reading travels over Bluetooth, and the app smooths it once more with a
filter of its own before it beeps. In a narrow core that difference is the moment you would have
turned.

We make both ends, so we could close that gap instead of living with it. The app does not wait for
a finished vario reading. It takes the instrument's own pressure readings and runs on them the same
filter, the same averaging and the same decision about when to beep that the instrument runs
inside. So the phone and the instrument hear the same thermal at the same moment and beep together,
practically in unison. And when you apply a sound from the list, it goes to the instrument and the
phone plays it too.

That gives the phone a real job in the cockpit. A piezo disc is a small bell with one note of its
own, and as the [piezo article](/blog/vario-piezo-tone-measured) measured, it cannot play the note it
is asked for very cleanly. A phone speaker can. On the phone the curve plays the actual pitches, the
octaves come out where they belong, and it is much louder, which matters in a helmet in the wind.
The instrument stays the source of truth and keeps beeping on its own if the phone is in a pocket;
the phone, when you want it, is the bigger voice of the same vario.

## A heart that only pilots see

The list now has a rating. Next to each entry there can be a heart with a number: pilots who like a
sound vote for it, and after a season the numbers should say which sounds people actually keep in
the air. That is worth more than any argument about curves.

Only pilots who fly with a FlyBeeper can vote. The hearts are there for owners of a FANET Vario or
a Sun Vario with firmware 0.32.0 or later, and a vote comes from the instrument itself, so a rating
cannot be pushed by people who have never flown the sound. There is nothing to set up: connect the
instrument to the app and the hearts appear. Everyone else sees the same list without hearts and
without numbers.

For now the rating lives in the FlyBeeper map app, on the phone and in the browser. The
configurator does not have it yet; it may come there later.

## What comes next

The list is fixed for now. The next step is your own sounds in it. A profile you have tuned and saved
as yours could go into the list for everyone, and then the hearts start to do their job: sorting the
list so that the sounds pilots keep end up near the top.

If you fly with a sound that is not here, tell us what it is. Every entry in this list started as
someone's sound in their head, and with a FlyBeeper it can stay there.
