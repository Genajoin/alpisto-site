The [previous article](/blog/how-a-flybeeper-vario-decides-when-to-beep) was about when a vario
beeps: the averaging, two thresholds on each side, a memory of which tone is playing and an early
exit. It ended with seven presets for those thresholds. This one is about the other half, how the
beep sounds, and about what happened when both halves went into one list.

The reason is simple. A pilot who has flown five years with one vario has that vario's sound in
their head. They know what +0.5 sounds like and what +3 sounds like without looking. A new vario
with a different curve is a new language in the one moment you least want to learn one. So the
FlyBeeper app now lets you pick a ready sound, including sounds that are as close as I could get to
the varios and flight apps people already fly with.

In the previous article I left the brand names out of the table: the point there was the numbers.
Here the names are the point. They belong to their owners, nobody of them endorsed anything below,
and every entry says where its numbers come from.

## One list instead of three

Until now the sound settings had a strip of tone curves, a grid of "When it sounds" presets and two
separate menus for your own profiles. They are now one list in the Sound section of a device's
settings in the app, in four groups: our own sounds, the sounds of other varios and apps, presets
from the community, and threshold sets on their own.

An entry carries a tone curve, thresholds, or both. Above the list a switch says what a choice
changes: everything, only the sound, or only when it sounds. So you can take the climb tone of one
vario and the thresholds of another, and the app recognises the two parts separately: the line
under the list reads like "Sound: SkyDrop · When it sounds: Sniffer". Own brings back the values
you had before you started clicking. A star marks a favourite. Tap an entry and its
description opens right under it: what it sounds like, the thresholds in numbers and the source.

Everything runs on the same twelve-point curve the previous article described: for each of twelve
vario values, a pitch, a period and a share of the period that sounds. Between the points the vario
draws straight lines. Whatever another vario does, it has to fit into that, and the sections below
are mostly about what fits and what does not.

## Where the numbers come from

Some sources are exact. XC Tracer publishes its tone table in every manual, in a format that is
almost ours: twelve tones, each a climb rate, a pitch, a period and a duty. Both the current factory
table and the older Mini 2016 one went in as they are. SkyDrop, XCSoar, GNUVario, BlueFly and Leaf
are open source, so their curves come from the code. The four community presets, Tim Pentreath's,
the lu-glidz sniffer, Matthss's Flymaster-like template and JP Philippe's, are files that people
share for the XC Tracer editor.

Others are partly rebuilt, and the app marks them so. The Flymaster manual gives the climb pitch as
a formula and the rhythm only as a chart, which I digitised. The Skytraxx tone editor and the Syride
configurator show their defaults on screen but publish no table. For the threshold sets, Gaggle
publishes its three, and Digifly names its defaults in the manual.

Two things never come across. Most other varios take a table longer than twelve points; where one
did, I dropped the points whose loss changes the curve least and kept the error in the notes. And
nobody states their averaging in seconds. Every manual has its own damping scale, so the brand
entries leave the averaging as it is on your device.

## XCTrack, measured from a screen recording

XCTrack is on a great many pilots' phones, and its default sound is not published anywhere. Its manual describes it in words: the pitch rises linearly up to 6 m/s, the
beeps get faster, a double beep in weak lift, a double sink alarm whose second tone drops a semitone
for every 0.5 m/s. No numbers.

The app has a sound page with a slider that plays the vario sound for any value from −7 to +7 m/s.
So I recorded the phone's screen with the sound while moving the slider slowly from one end to the
other, 38 seconds. In each frame I found the slider's knob; in the audio I found each beep, its
pitch, its length and the time to the next one. Then the knob's position had to become metres per
second, and that is less obvious than it looks: the slider is not linear. Its scale is sixteen
columns, −7, −5, −4, −3, −2, −1, 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7, spread evenly, so the
half metre around zero takes as much room as the four metres from +3 to +7. With that scale the
recording checks itself: the sink sound stops exactly at −2.0 and the first climb beep comes at
+0.09, and the phone's settings say −2.0 and +0.1.

What it plays, with the default settings:

<div style="overflow-x:auto">

| Zone | Sound |
|---|---|
| Climb, from +0.1 | A beep of 100 ms whatever the climb, the pitch fixed within a beep. Pitch about 659 + 82 Hz per m/s, from 660 Hz at zero to 1238 Hz at +7. Period about 500 − 40 ms per m/s, from 495 ms to 215 ms. |
| Weak lift, about −0.35 to +0.1 | Two short beeps at 330 Hz, 400 ms apart, once a second, the same whatever the value. |
| Silence | From −2.0 to about −0.35. |
| Sink, below −2.0 | Once a second a short 660 Hz beep, then a low tone that drops a semitone for each 0.5 m/s and gets longer: 523 Hz for 260 ms at −2, 330 Hz for 490 ms at −5 and below. |

</div>

The tones of the sink alarm and of the weak lift are exact notes of the scale, C down to E. The
climb pitch rises more gently than the manual's octave up to 6 m/s: at +6 it is about 1150 Hz, not
1320. And the sound lags the slider by about half a second, although the averaging is set to 0.1 s,
so the app smooths its sound somewhere it does not show.

Two of these things do not fit a FlyBeeper. Our curve cannot play two different tones in one period,
so the sink alarm became its low tone alone, once a second at the same pitch and length. And the
weak lift double beep cannot be a double beep. It became two entries: XCTrack, without the weak lift
tone, and XCTrack Weak lift, where the climb tone starts at −0.35 and the band up to +0.1 plays single
330 Hz beeps every half second, as many beeps as XCTrack plays, just evenly spaced.

## SeeYou Navigator, with a logarithmic scale

The SeeYou Navigator entry is close to the Oudie sound with its default logarithmic scale. Naviter's
own help explains the idea: with a logarithmic scale the pitch changes fastest near zero, so the
first few tenths of a climb are the easiest to hear apart. The entry plays 600 Hz at zero, 1063 Hz
at +1 and 2200 Hz at +10, with a long beep, three quarters of every period.

Naviter publishes no numbers for its defaults, so the climb part is close but not official, and the
sink part is mine. A steady sink tone fits what the help says: beeping in sink is a separate mode
there, the Pace-maker. So below −2.5 the entry plays a continuous tone that falls the same way the
climb tone rises, fastest near the threshold: 400 Hz at −2.5, 345 at −4, 300 at −6, 260 at −10. The
threshold of −2.5 is a guess as well; the help only says the vario is silent between zero and the
value you set.

## Simple

The first entry in the list is ours, and it is the one I would give a pilot who has no sound in
their head yet. It keeps the three zones that most varios share, a steady sink tone, quiet ticks
near zero and climb beeps, and gives each zone one rule instead of hand-tuned points.

The climb starts at 600 Hz and goes 150 Hz higher for each metre per second, up to 1050 Hz at +3,
then flattens to 1550 Hz at +10. That keeps the whole climb on the part of our buzzer where loudness
is even, below the dip around 1600–1800 Hz that the [piezo article](/blog/vario-piezo-tone-measured)
measured. The beep is always half of the period, and the period goes from 600 ms to 150 ms, the same
rhythm nearly every vario on the market uses. The sink tone is steady and falls with the sink, from
580 Hz to 400 Hz. The ticks near zero, from −0.5 to +0.04, sit below the climb tone's hold of +0.05,
so with Simple's own thresholds they never sound; pick Sniffer or Flatland under When it sounds and
they do.

The thresholds are what most of the market does: climb from +0.1, held to +0.05, sink below −2.5, no
early exit. The averaging, 0.3 s, is calmer than our factory 0.1 s and gives fewer false beeps in
choppy air, without the lag of the 0.6 s in Mountain.

## A heart that only pilots see

Next to each entry of the list there can be a heart with a number. It is a vote: I like this sound.
After a season the numbers should say which sounds pilots actually keep, and that is worth more
than any argument about curves.

A vote in an app is easy to fake, and a rating that anyone can push with a script is worse than
none. So a vote here needs two things that a script does not have: a FlyBeeper in your hand and a
person behind it.

The first is the instrument. Starting with firmware 0.32.0, each FANET and Sun vario makes itself a
key of its own. The secret half never leaves the chip. When the app connects, the server
sends a random challenge, the instrument signs it, and the server checks the signature against the
instrument's public key. That proves a real FlyBeeper with current firmware is next to the phone,
and nothing else. The Mini BT has no such key and cannot vote for now. A small detail on the way: browsers do not let a web page read a Bluetooth
device's serial number, it is on their block list, so the instrument hands its serial over together
with the key.

The second is the person. The app is linked to Telegram through our bot, the same link that carries
safety alerts and flyability notices. One Telegram account is one voter, whatever the number of
instruments, and one account can bring up to three of them. An instrument belongs to one account;
if yours changes hands, write to us.

None of it asks you to do anything. Connect your instrument with a linked app and the app gets a
pass for 30 days, renewed whenever you connect again. With the pass, hearts appear in the list.
Without it, there are no hearts and no numbers at all, not even other people's: the list looks the
same as before, so nobody reads a count as advice from people they cannot ask. The server keeps the
serial and public key of each instrument, a pseudonym in place of the Telegram account, made with a key
only the server holds, and the votes. No names and no chat.

## What comes next

The list is fixed for now: the entries above, in the order above. The next step is your own sounds in
it. A profile you have tuned and saved as yours could go into the list for everyone, and then the
hearts start to do their job: sorting the list so that the sounds pilots keep end up near the top.

If you fly with a sound that is not here and you know its numbers, or you can record it the way I
recorded XCTrack, send it. Every entry in this list started as someone's sound in their head.
