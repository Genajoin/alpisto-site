Every pilot knows that a thermal drifts with the wind. You climb, you lose the core, and it turns out
to be a little downwind of where you left it. The rule of thumb is to move your circle into the wind
side of the drift and keep following it.

I needed a number rather than a rule. The thermal assistant in the FlyBeeper app remembers where you
climbed well and where you did not, and to use those points it has to know where each of them has
gone since — how far the air they were in has moved. My first measurements from my own tracks gave
"about 0.6 of the wind". Measurements across several pilots said something else: 0.3 near the slope,
0.7 higher up. Both were done carefully. Both could not be the whole story.

The trouble is that one pilot measures two things at once and cannot tell them apart. The centre you
circle around moves for two reasons. The column may **lean**: climb a hundred metres, and the core at
your new height is somewhere downwind of where it was. And the column may **drift**: wait a minute,
and the whole thing has moved. A climbing pilot does both at the same time, so a single track gives
you only the sum.

To separate them you need several pilots in the same thermal, in the same minute, at different
heights. That is exactly what a competition start looks like.

## A hundred pilots in one thermal

Competition scoring servers publish every task's tracks. [airscore.fai.org](https://airscore.fai.org)
has the IGC files of each scored task as one archive. I took eleven tasks from nine competitions in
eight countries — Slovenia, North Macedonia, Serbia, Turkey, Italy, Brazil, the Czech Republic and
Utah — plus my own days at Kobarid. Nearly all files come from Flymaster instruments: one fix per
second, with a live barometer. That is a lot of pilots circling in the same air: 217 on one day at
Kobarid, 133 at Kruševo.

Pilots' names play no part in any of this and do not appear anywhere below.

## From circles to an axis

Every full turn of a pilot gives one point: *here, at this height, at this moment, was the middle of
the circle*. The mean position over one full turn is exactly the centre of the circle as drawn in the
air, at the middle of the turn — the wind drift of the glider itself cancels out.

Then, for every thermal, I fit a straight axis that is allowed to lean and to move:

<div style="overflow-x:auto">

$$
c(z, t) = c_0 + B\,(z - \bar z) + V\,(t - \bar t)
$$

</div>

Here $c$ is the centre of the circle, $z$ the height and $t$ the time.

$B$ is the lean, in metres sideways per metre of height. $V$ is the drift of the whole column, in
metres per second. A lone climbing pilot sees $V + B\cdot\text{climb}$ and nothing else. With pilots
at different heights in the same minute, $B$ and $V$ come apart.

Physics suggests two limiting cases. A **plume** from a fixed source on the ground stays where it is
and is laid over by the wind: air rising at $w$ in a wind $W$ travels $W/w$ metres sideways for every
metre up, so $B \approx W/w$ and $V \approx 0$. A **bubble** that has left the ground stands upright
and goes with the air around it: $B \approx 0$, $V$ close to the wind.

A big start gaggle is not one thermal. At Kobarid on 27 August 2023, a simple clustering of circle
centres glued 210 pilots over an hour and three kilometres into one "thermal". So the gaggle is cut
into ten-minute slices, and in each slice a sequential RANSAC looks for the most populated axis —
every circle within 110 m of it — takes its circles out, and looks for the next one. A thermal counts
if it has at least three pilots, 250 m of height, pilots at different heights within the same minute,
and some wind to lean in. Circle centres scatter around the fitted axis by 40–65 m, which is about a
circle's radius.

That leaves 865 thermals.

## Three thermals you can turn around

The same three from the side, looking across the wind. Each dot is the centre of one circle,
coloured by time. The three lines are the fitted axis at the beginning, the middle and the end of the
thermal's life, each in the colour of the dots of that moment. The lean shows in every line; the
drift is the gap between them.

![Three thermals seen across the wind: Kobarid, Aksaray, Niš](/img/blog/thermal-axis-from-competition-tracks/three-regimes.png)

At **Kobarid** the three lines lie on top of each other: the column does not move at all. It is laid
over at 45°, parallel to the slope and about a hundred metres above it, and the lower pilots circle a
good three hundred metres upwind of the higher ones. Extended downwards, the axis meets the ground on
the slope — where this thermal leaves it.

At **Niš**, 1600 m above the plain, it is the other way round. The column stands almost upright, and
the whole stack of 82 pilots slides downwind — 1.3 km in eight minutes, at 0.8 of the wind.

**Aksaray**, in 5 m/s of wind 500 m over a plateau, does a little of both.

## The numbers

Sorted by how high the middle of the axis is above the terrain under it:

<div style="overflow-x:auto">

| Axis above the terrain | Thermals | Lean along the wind, m/m | Lean across the wind (control) | Column drift, × wind | What a climbing pilot sees, × wind |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| below 300 m | 146 | **+1.03** | +0.03 | **0.14** | 0.67 |
| 300–600 m | 152 | +0.48 | +0.01 | 0.49 | 0.78 |
| 600–900 m | 143 | +0.19 | +0.04 | 0.63 | 0.81 |
| 900–1500 m | 341 | +0.18 | 0.00 | 0.64 | 0.78 |
| above 1500 m | 83 | −0.02 | +0.05 | **0.67** | 0.75 |

</div>

Medians. The spread is wide — the middle half of the thermals near the ground lean anywhere from 0.5
to 1.5 m per metre — so these describe the typical thermal, not every one.

![Lean, drift and what a climbing pilot sees, by height above the terrain](/img/blog/thermal-axis-from-competition-tracks/by-height.png)

Near the terrain the thermal behaves like a plume: it stays put and the wind lays it over. The ratio
of the measured lean to the wind-over-climb that a plume should have is, in the median, **1.00**. From
600 m up it turns into a column that is carried along with two thirds of the wind and leans only a
little. Above 1500 m the lean is gone.

The last column is the interesting one. Drift plus lean times climb comes out at 0.67–0.8 of the wind
at every height. Near the slope it is mostly lean; high up it is mostly drift; the pilot in the core
cannot feel the difference. That settles my contradiction. The 0.6 from my own tracks was this sum.
The 0.3 near the slope from the several-pilot measurement was mostly the drift of the column, because
pooling many pilots at many heights averages the lean away.

## The wind, or the slope?

Near the ground most of these thermals sit over a slope, and air flowing up a warm slope follows it.
A thermal leaning parallel to the hillside might be doing just that, with the wind playing no part.

It can be checked. Where the wind blows *down* the slope under the thermal — 153 cases with a slope
steeper than 10 % — a slope-following column would lean uphill, into the wind. It does not: it leans
downwind by 0.45 m per metre, and so leans *away* from the hill, by 0.32 m per metre.

<div style="overflow-x:auto">

| Wind relative to the slope under the axis | Thermals | Lean along the wind | Lean up the slope |
|---|---|---|---|
| blowing up the slope | 223 | +0.48 | +0.36 |
| across the slope | 174 | +0.48 | +0.08 |
| blowing down the slope | 153 | +0.45 | −0.32 |

</div>

The lean follows the wind whichever way the hill faces. And across the wind there is no lean at all:
in every height band the sideways component is zero, spread evenly on both sides.

![Lean along the wind against lean across the wind](/img/blog/thermal-axis-from-competition-tracks/along-vs-across.png)

## Could the method invent the lean?

A RANSAC that picks the most populated straight line out of a cloud of points could, in principle,
find slopes that are not there. Two checks.

First, I shuffled the heights of the circles inside every cluster — keeping positions and times,
breaking any link between height and position — and ran the whole pipeline again. The lean came out
at **0.00** at every height, and the drift took up all the motion, as it should.

Second, the knobs. A tolerance of 80, 110 or 150 m around the axis and slices of 6, 10 or 15 minutes
give 0.95–1.00 m per metre near the ground and 0.16–0.20 above 600 m. The numbers do not depend on
the settings.

## What it changes

**For a pilot under a gaggle.** Near the terrain, the core at your height is upwind of the pilots
above you — by roughly the height difference. A gaggle 300 m higher near the slope marks a point about
300 m downwind of where you should be looking. High above the ground the core is almost right under
them, but it has drifted since they found it.

**For the app.** One drift factor that depends on the height above the terrain is not enough. The
assistant needs two: a lean and a drift, both depending on that height. The rule the app uses today
was taken from the several-pilot measurement: 0.3 of the wind near the slope. For your own climb near
the slope that is about half of what you actually see. When other pilots are visible on the radio
(FANET), the lean also gives a direct hint — *the core at your height is here* — which is on my list.

**For a map of thermal sources.** For thermals that stay put, the axis extended to the ground is where
the thermal leaves it. Collected over many days, that is a map of house thermals with the direction
they lean on a given wind.

## Limits

- **The axis is a straight line.** Within a ten-minute slice the model is linear, while the lean
  clearly changes with height. The line is an average over the curve.
- **The low thermals are Alpine.** Almost everything below 300 m above the ground comes from the
  slopes of Kobarid and Cuorgnè. Low thermals over flat ground are missing, and they may behave
  differently.
- **The climb of the air is rough.** It is the pilot's climb plus a fixed allowance for the glider's
  sink in a turn, so "wind over climb" is good to about ±30 %.
- **Competition days are good days.** Racing pilots fly strong, busy thermals after the start;
  a weak, lonely thermal at 5 pm is not in this sample.
- The calm French Open task did not produce a single usable thermal: without wind there is nothing to
  lean in, and the method needs some.
